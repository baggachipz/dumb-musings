This experiment demonstrates a real phenomenon in time perception: your brain adapts to a delay between an action and its sensory consequence, and then misjudges what happened first.

What you'll do

  1. Press SPACE a few times to get into a rhythm.
  2. Baseline measurement. On each trial, press as fast as you can when told, then judge whether the flash or your press came first.
  3. Adaptation. Keep pressing while every flash is secretly delayed by 200 ms.
  4. Measurement again, with the delay maintained.
  5. The delay is removed, and you see what that feels like.

Takes about 5 minutes. Use a physical keyboard on a desktop or laptop, and avoid other activity while it runs.

The lines are fitted psychometric curves, and each vertical marker is that block's point of subjective simultaneity — the flash timing you were equally likely to call either way.

What's going on?

Your brain continually predicts the sensory consequences of your actions. When a delay is inserted between your keypress and the flash, it recalibrates: it starts treating the delayed flash as the normal consequence of pressing.

The measurement is the point of subjective simultaneity (PSS): the physical flash timing at which you call it either way half the time. Recalibration pushes that point later — a flash has to arrive further after your press to feel simultaneous with it. Once that has happened, removing the delay can make the flash feel like it preceded the press that caused it.

Perception is not a timestamp recorder. Your brain combines sensory signals with predictions about when those signals ought to arrive.
